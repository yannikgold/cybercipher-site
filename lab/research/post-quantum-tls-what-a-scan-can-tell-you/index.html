Post-Quantum TLS in the Wild: What an Endpoint Scan Can and Cannot Tell You

Yannik Goldgräbe · 10 Nov 2026 · 7 min read
AbstractHybrid key exchange has quietly become the default in browsers and at large edge networks. A scan of a public endpoint shows one specific thing — whether the handshake still leaks a classically-protected secret — and it is worth being precise about what that does and does not mean.

Post-quantum migration has a public surface and a private one. The public surface is the TLS handshake: it is what a browser negotiates, what a load balancer terminates, and what anyone on the network path can record today and try to decrypt later. The private surface is everything behind it — certificates, signing keys, stored data, internal protocols. An endpoint scan only sees the first. That makes it useful, and it makes it easy to over-read.

This article sets out what a scan of a public TLS endpoint actually measures, why the key exchange moved first, and where the remaining exposure sits. It is the reasoning behind TLS Quantum Scan, the Lab's readiness checker, and it applies equally to any other tool that reports on the same handshake.

Why key exchange moved first

The threat that forces action now is not a quantum computer breaking live sessions. It is recording: an adversary captures encrypted traffic today and decrypts it when a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists. For a classical TLS 1.3 session, the session keys derive from an elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman exchange. Break that one secret later, and every recorded byte of the session opens. Confidentiality has a retroactive failure mode; authentication does not.

That asymmetry is why the industry prioritised the key exchange. Authentication — the certificate chain and the handshake signature — only has to hold at the moment of the handshake. A forged signature in the future cannot alter a connection that already finished. Certificates can therefore migrate later, and they will, because post-quantum signatures are large and the certificate ecosystem is slow to move.

What “hybrid” means in the handshake

TLS 1.3 negotiates key exchange through the key_share extension, where client and server agree on a named group. The deployed post-quantum groups are hybrids: a classical exchange and a lattice-based key encapsulation run side by side, and the resulting shared secrets are concatenated before key derivation. The widely deployed combination pairs X25519 with ML-KEM-768, the key encapsulation mechanism standardised by NIST in FIPS 203.

The hybrid is a deliberate hedge. If ML-KEM turns out to have a flaw, the session is still as strong as X25519. If a quantum computer breaks X25519, the session is still as strong as ML-KEM. The cost is a larger ClientHello — the ML-KEM-768 public key alone is about 1.2 kilobytes — which is why some middleboxes and older TLS stacks that assumed a single-packet hello had to be fixed before this could be turned on by default.

What a scan measures

A scan opens connections to the endpoint and observes the negotiation. The informative results are narrow and concrete: which key-exchange groups the server accepts, which one it chooses when a client offers both hybrid and classical groups, whether it still allows TLS 1.2 (which has no post-quantum key exchange path at all), and which signature algorithms and certificate types appear in the chain.

From that, three honest categories follow. The endpoint negotiates a hybrid group by preference: recorded traffic from modern clients is protected against later decryption. It supports a hybrid group but prefers a classical one: protection depends on what each client offers and the server's selection logic. It offers no post-quantum group at all: every session is recordable and decryptable later, regardless of how modern the client is.

What a scan cannot see

  • —Certificates and signatures. Every public endpoint today authenticates with classical signatures. That is expected and is not an immediate risk, but a scan cannot tell you whether the operator has a plan for when it becomes one.
  • —The other side of the terminator. A hybrid handshake at the edge says nothing about the TLS between the load balancer and the application, the database connections, the message queues, or the service mesh. Internal traffic is often recorded for longer and migrated last.
  • —Data at rest. Backups, archives, and encrypted storage keyed by RSA or ECC envelopes are the longest-lived exposure and are invisible from the network.
  • —Non-TLS protocols. SSH, IPsec, VPNs, and proprietary protocols each have their own migration path and their own timeline.
  • —Cryptographic inventory. A scan tells you what one endpoint does. Readiness is a property of the whole system, and the system has to be enumerated before it can be migrated.

Reading the result as an architect

The practical use of a scan is as a sampling instrument. Run it across every externally reachable endpoint, and the distribution tells you how much of your perimeter already benefits from hybrid key exchange for free — usually most of it, if it sits behind a modern CDN or edge — and which endpoints are self-managed and lagging. Those are the ones to fix, and the fix is typically a library or configuration update, not a redesign.

The harder work begins where the scan stops. Certificates will need a path to ML-DSA or to a hybrid signature scheme once the ecosystem settles on one. Long-lived data needs re-keying under post-quantum envelopes. Internal protocols need the same treatment the edge already received. None of that is visible from outside, and all of it is why post-quantum readiness remains an architecture problem rather than a checkbox on a scan report.

Key insight. A green scan result means one thing: the session secret is no longer derived from a classical exchange alone. Treat it as the easiest layer, verified — not as the migration, finished.
